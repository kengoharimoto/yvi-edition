# YVi on āgama and upamāna (E 30,9 – 33,9): draft translation

Draft by Claude, 2026-09-14, from the draft transcript at the end of `text07.tex` (not yet in apparatus markup).
Bhāṣya words are in **bold**. Square brackets supply what the Sanskrit leaves implicit; a question mark marks
a rendering that depends on an uncertain reading. Textual notes are collected at the end.

## The Bhāṣya (YBh 1.7)

**An object seen or inferred by a reliable person is taught by words to another, in order to transfer his understanding. The hearer's mental function, arising from the words and having their object as its object, is testimony. The testimony whose speaker has an unreliable object, one neither seen nor inferred, wavers. But when the original speaker has seen or inferred the object, it is free from wavering.**

## E 30,9

**A reliable person** (āpta) is one impelled by the wish to benefit others, free from faults, who has seen or inferred the object. The object seen or inferred by him [is taught] **to another**, to a particular hearer, **in order to transfer his own understanding**, that is, to convey his own knowledge, **by words**. "Words" means a sentence, since a specific meaning is understood from it. Hence the mental function that has as its object the meaning of the sentence taught by him is, like inference, chiefly concerned with ascertaining the general.

## E 30,14

Thus testimony, which presupposes the grasping of the speaker's qualities such as reliability, belongs to **the hearer**, not to the speaker. For the speaker has seen or inferred the object, and his knowledge of it is inferential or perceptual. It is the hearer's alone: the ascertained cognition that presupposes hearing and is distinct from the inferential and the perceptual, that is testimony. It is not perception, because [its object] is not within the range of the senses; nor is it inference, because it does not depend on the relation of mark and mark-bearer as described. For a cognition connected with the meaning of words such as "heaven", "apūrva", "deity" does not arise preceded by an already acquired relation of mark and mark-bearer. The result, however, is in every case only the person's awareness of the mental function.

## E 30,20

**Whose** … the speaker … [lacuna in E] … Since the mental function called testimony arises from the utterance of words, testimony has [already] been conveyed by the presentation of an instance, and its result is as before. And even when there is only a single instance, still, because only the bare relation of what is to be established and what establishes it is intended, no [fault arises] from observing the converse of the negative [concomitance]. So it is said. (?)

## E 30,23

Testimony itself is [here] specified as what is stated by a speaker whose object is unreliable. How is he a speaker of an unreliable object? To this he says: **the object has not been seen or inferred** by him, the speaker, such as the Buddha or the Arhat, because they teach otherwise than what is well established among cultivated people. For one whose inner organ is not impaired by faults such as passion does not describe what is well established otherwise. **That testimony wavers**, that is, it becomes a mere semblance [of testimony].

## E 30,27

Although the display of the semblances that fall under perception and inference is not made in so many words, still, by the very display of the semblance of testimony it is said by implication that in the earlier cases too the semblance of perception, the semblance of inference and so on are to be recognized as what runs contrary to the definitions as stated.

## E 31,1

**But when the original speaker**, the first speaker, the Lord, [has seen or inferred the object], the testimony that has him as its cause would be **free from wavering**, because there is no cause of wavering, like [the testimony of] a speaker who speaks in accordance with the object.

## E 31,3: comparison is not a separate means of knowledge

Comparison presupposes words; because it falls within testimony it is not a separate means of knowledge. For example: for one who has ascertained the resemblance from the very sentence spoken by the forester, "a gavaya is like a cow", when he later sees a gavaya in the forest there is only the recollection of the resemblance brought to mind by the sentence heard before, nothing more. The resemblance itself had already been apprehended from the sentence alone.

## E 31,7

Nor is it a means of knowledge merely because the relation between name and named is apprehended, since that too is understood from that very sentence. For one who has not learned the name "gavaya" does not, on the strength of [the word] "cow" alone, gain the understanding of the relation between name and named, "this is a gavaya", even when he sees a gavaya in the forest.

## E 31,10

Therefore in "a gavaya is like a cow" the word "gavaya" too must be expected in that sentence. And that being so, everything is apprehended from what was heard before. And if it were [objected] that "this is a gavaya" is not the object of the earlier cognition, that too, on the part of those honourable people who see no way out of the objection, looks like a harassed dog taking its stand in a corner. (?)

## E 31,14

For then, through differences of nearness and remoteness in place and time and the like, a multiplicity of comparisons would follow. If [you say] "let it be so": that is already covered by the earlier cognition, which contains all the particulars within it, [as] has been shown before. (?) For when "cow" is said, it is not the case that, in the absence of particulars of place, time and so on, the object is not understood from the word "cow".

## E 31,17

Moreover, the apprehension of the relation between name and named is not comparison. For comparison is that by which something is compared, and that is the knowledge of resemblance. Nothing is compared through the apprehension of the relation between named and name, because that comes later: it is after one has ascertained the resemblance that this [judgement] too, "this is a gavaya", falls into place.

## E 31,21

In that case there would be a separate means of knowledge not preceded by words, such as the villager's cognition on seeing a gavaya, "a cow is like this". That too is not [a separate means of knowledge], because there is no object of knowledge. For a means of knowledge, which is held to apprehend what exists, operates only when there is an object of knowledge. But in the cow and the gavaya there is in reality no single thing called "resemblance" that is [at once] distinct [from them] and present [in both].

## E 31,25

[Objection:] But the cognition of resemblance certainly exists, and it cannot be without an objective basis.

[Reply:] True. This is exactly what the inquiry turns on: is it like the notion of a forest, a herd and the like, or like the cognition of a pot? And what follows from this? If it were like the notion of a forest, then just as trees alone, standing in a particular arrangement, are the cause of the notion of a forest, which does not exist apart from them, in the same way the parts of the cow and the gavaya alone, in dependence on the sight [of the one] and the memory [of the other], would be the cause of the apprehension of a resemblance that does not exist apart from themselves. And so unreality [of resemblance] results.

## E 32,1

If, on the other hand, [it is] like the cognition of a pot, then [resemblance] would be a real thing and [its cognition] a means of knowledge.

## E 32,2

Here it is examined what kind of thing it would be. Is the resemblance a single thing present in both cow and gavaya, other than cowness and gavaya-ness? Or is it just those two [universals] as qualifying each other? Or two resemblances, distinct from those two, residing in cow and gavaya [respectively], either independent of each other or mutually dependent? Is it the individual itself as qualified by the other's universal? Or the universal as qualified by the other individual, or by both individuals? Or a relation among these, as the alternatives permit? And many further alternatives are to be raised by way of each qualifying the other and so on.

Among these: if the resemblance is one, located in both, then there is no being an object of comparison, because of oneness, as in "a cow is like a cow".

## E 32,10

If [you say] one of the two individuals is compared by means of it, that is not so either, because [the resemblance] belongs to its own self; [one cannot say] "like a cow" by virtue of cowness. If [you say] one individual is compared with another individual that possesses it, not this either, for cowness is not the cause of the comparability of the hornless cow and the broken-horned cow. If [you say] the resemblance causes that cognition in dependence on a further property, then the resemblance is lost: let that very property be [the cause] of that cognition. What use is your wretched invented resemblance?

## E 32,14

Moreover, that property too, which is depended upon: is it one, in both, like the resemblance, or different? Such alternatives would arise, so the impossibility is as before. If a distinct resemblance is spoken of, that is not [right] either, precisely because of the distinctness; for it does not happen that "a horse is like a cow". Just as there is unfitness on account of [the resemblance] being one [in each], so too when many [properties] are depended upon. By this the alternatives formulated earlier, universal, individual, relation and so on, are answered.

## E 32,19

If [you say] resemblance is posited because the arising of the cognition cannot otherwise be accounted for, then why, in the same way, is reality not posited for the notions of forest, hollow, east, south, north and the like, since [they are] experienced and cannot otherwise be accounted for? You might think that the trees and the rest are there the causes of the arising of those notions. If so, "a cow is like a gavaya" is a false cognition, like the notion of a forest. If [the causes are the parts] that are distinct, what is the point of comparison here? [It would be like] "a horse is like a cow". If both, the parts, being of the nature of the distinct and of the common, are the causes of that cognition, even so it comes to the same as the notion of a forest.

## E 32,25

Therefore, under examination it does not withstand much pounding. So the very parts, perceived in both in large number, are the cause of the cognition of resemblance, as trees are of the notion of a forest. Hence it is established that there is no separate means of knowledge called comparison.

## E 32,27

Nor is a duality of means of knowledge to be posited from a duality of objects. Why? Because the object is apprehended according to the means of knowledge; a means of knowledge is not posited according to the object. And if a means of knowledge were posited according to the object, then now … [lacuna in E] … that is not possible, because those who know are not omniscient. In "from worshipping a caitya heaven results", the cognition that caitya-worship is a cause of heaven would be false, because it is not the object of perception or inference; or else another means of knowledge would have to be posited for it.

## E 33,7

If [you say] that cognition is the result of inference, then the result would arise elsewhere than from the means of knowledge [claimed]; and likewise the cognition of the meaning of sentences such as "… the three instruments …" (?).

## E 33,9

Nor is the intention here to define the means of knowledge, since [the passage] aims only at indicating the means of restraining the well-known mental functions, those of the means of knowledge and the rest. For this very reason the author of the sūtras did not compose a sūtra [defining them]. The author of the Bhāṣya, however, relied on nothing but a restatement of what is well known, and following him we have chattered a little. [Thus ends the commentary on sūtras] 6–7.

## Textual notes

Corrupt or doubtful places that affect the translation:

1. E 30,20 *yasyāgamasya vaktā … nimitte*: E prints dots; the construction cannot be recovered from the transcript. Tm is partly in lacuna here.
2. E 31,1 *yathā-artha(ya)vaktṛvat*: read *yathārthavaktṛvat*.
3. E 31,3 *pramṇāntaram*: *pramāṇāntaram*; *avadhṛtasādṛśya punar*: *avadhṛtasādṛśyasya punar* (the genitive is needed).
4. E 31,10 *pīḍitakaivale[kaule]yakavṛttikoṇo 'vasthānam*: adopt E's *kauleyaka* (dog); the image is a cornered dog.
5. E 31,14 *antarnihitasamastaviśeṣam eva hi vā neyau pūrvam upapāditam*: corrupt; something like *evaṃ hi vākyam iti pūrvam upapāditam* is wanted.
6. E 32,3 *parasparānupekḍe*: *parasparānapekṣe*.
7. E 32,11 *gotvvaṃ*: *gotvaṃ*.
8. E 32,22 *vyāvṛttanuvṛttātmānas*: *vyāvṛttānuvṛttātmānas*; *tadbudhdihetavaḥ*: *tadbuddhihetavaḥ*.
9. E 32,25 *parikṣyamāṇaṃ*: *parīkṣyamāṇaṃ*; *prmāṇāntaram*: *pramāṇāntaram*.
10. E 32,27 *premeyadvitvāt*: *prameyadvitvāt*.
11. E 33,4 *adhunā* followed by a gap in E: the apodosis is lost.
12. E 33,7 *anmānaphalam*: *anumānaphalam*; *canirmokṣaḥ(ka)raṇatrayādivākyārthaviṣyabuddhir*: unresolved; perhaps a quoted sentence *nirmokṣaḥ …*.

14. E 31,24 Tm *vyāvṛttānu⟨pa⟩vṛttañ ca* against E *vyāvṛttam anuvṛttaṃ ca*: E's reading is right; Tm's *pa* is a slip.
