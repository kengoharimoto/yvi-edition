# YVi on the Bhāṣya's definition of anumāna (E 25,21 – 30,7): draft translation

Draft by Claude, 2026-09-14, from `text07.tex` as it stands. Bhāṣya words are in **bold**.
Square brackets supply what the Sanskrit leaves implicit. Textual suggestions are collected at the end.

## The Bhāṣya (YBh 1.7)

**Inference is the mental function whose object is the relation which, belonging to the thing to be inferred, is present in things of the same class and absent from things of a different class, and which is chiefly concerned with ascertaining the general. For instance: the moon and stars possess motion, because they reach another place, like Caitra; and the Vindhya is motionless, because it does not reach [another place].**

## E 25,21

Now inference is stated: **of the thing to be inferred … present in things of the same class**.

**Of the thing to be inferred**, that is, of the thing qualified by the property [to be inferred]. **Present in things of the same class**, [i.e. in things that are of the same class] by possessing that property; [present] by its mere existence [there]. **Absent from things of a different class**, that is, from things having a property contrary to it; the meaning is that it does not exist in them.

**Relation**: what relation, and between which two? For a relation does not attain the status of a relation without regard to its two relata. And yet it is spoken of as if already well known, by **which**.

## E 25,26

[Objection:] But since inference is the topic, the relation between the mark and the mark-bearer is well known indeed.

[Reply:] No. Because it does not make anything known, a mere relation is not what is called the maker-known (gamaka). [What is meant is] the relation of the mark itself, in respect of its presence and absence. What then is this? Just the mark. For nothing other than the mark is present [in like things] and absent [from unlike things], and that is what makes [the mark-bearer] known.

[Objection:] But the mark too makes nothing known if its relation to the mark-bearer is disregarded. And if a maker-known disregarded its relation to the mark-bearer, anything whatever would be a maker-known. Therefore the relation between mark and mark-bearer has to be stated.

## E 26,7

[Reply:] Not [so], because it has been stated: it has been stated by these very words, **present in things of the same class as the thing to be inferred**. For what is not connected with the thing to be inferred, the mark-bearer, neither persists in things of a similar class nor withdraws from things of a different class. Therefore **which**, too, merely restates a relation that is already well known.

## E 26,10

**Having that as its object**: the mental function whose object is the relatum thus qualified by positive and negative concomitance. **Chiefly concerned with ascertaining the general**: chiefly concerned with ascertaining the mark-bearer in general. [That is] **inference**.

## E 26,12

**For instance: the moon and stars possess motion, because they reach another place, like Caitra.** Although this [definition] is made clear by the mere statement of the definition, it becomes clearer still through the display of an example and a counter-example; hence the presentation of an example.

Here, in **the moon and stars possess motion**, the possession of motion by the moon and stars is the thing to be inferred. Reaching another place is present in things of the same class as it, namely in things that possess motion, such as Caitra, and is absent from things of a different class, namely from motionless things such as the Vindhya. Thus it does not deviate, because of its constant relation with what possesses motion. Therefore a relation of this kind, by invariable concomitance, makes the other relatum known, as [in the case of] victim and killer, … cause and effect, and so on.

## E 26,19

[Objection:] Granted that things which are not in conflict, such as reaching another place [and possessing motion], stand as the known and the maker-known because of their constant relation. But how [can this be] for victim and killer and the like, where there is no relation?

[Reply:] There too there is no fault, because there is a relation of known and maker-known, [that is,] because there is a constant relation. For here, too, no relation of the nature of contact is intended, since [otherwise] it would follow that there is no inference "from what is seen in general". For in the case of the moon and stars, no relation of the nature of contact between reaching another place [and possessing motion] is grasped by perception. And if something made known without being grasped, everything would make everything known; for there is nothing that is not connected with something, since space and the like exist everywhere.

## E 26,25

Therefore, in the domain of inference, a relation is simply what causes the arising of a cognition of the known from the maker-known. And so in the case of victim and killer too, when the absence of the one is seen the presence of the other is understood: this is inference and nothing else. So too [is the case of something] whose presence elsewhere is understood from seeing its absence in its proper place. For instance, from seeing the killer the absence of the victim is inferred, and when Caitra, who is alive, is seen to be absent from his house, his being outside [is inferred].

## E 27,1: absence and presumption are inference

[Objection:] But absence and presumption are not [inference], because the definition of inference does not fit them, as [it does not fit] a cow and the like.

[Reply:] No, because the definition as stated does fit. Between victim and killer there is a relation of the nature of opposition. Wherever the killer is present, there the victim is certainly absent: this is invariable concomitance. For the presence of the killer does not deviate from the absence of the victim, nor the absence of the victim from the presence of the killer, just as the presence of smoke does not deviate from the presence of fire.

## E 27,6

[Objection:] But how is it known that the cognition of the victim's absence in the killer's vicinity arises from the mere sight of the killer's presence, and not rather from the fact that the means of knowledge which apprehend what exists do not operate on the victim as they do on the killer?

[Reply:] It is said: the cognition of the victim's absence arises only for one whose means of knowledge is engaged on the presence of the killer, [just as] the cognition of the presence of fire arises only for one whose means of knowledge is engaged on the presence of smoke. Likewise, for one whose means of knowledge is engaged on a particular killer there arises the cognition of the absence of a particular victim, [just as] the cognition of a particular fire arises only for one whose means of knowledge is engaged on a particular smoke.

## E 27,12

But for one who holds absence to be a means of knowledge, since the absence of a means of knowledge is undifferentiated everywhere, it is impossible that the seeing of the absence of the one opposed [term] should conform to the seeing of a particular opposing [term], in the way that the seeing of a particular mark-bearer conforms to a particular mark. For the non-operation of the means of knowledge that apprehend what exists has no particularity by which a particular cognition could conform to that particularity.

## E 27,16

If [you say that the particular cognition arises] because the means of knowledge that apprehend what exists are absent with respect to the particular thing one wishes to know, [we answer:] no, because it is impossible for a non-entity to depend on the co-presence of memory, desire and the like. For a non-entity is never seen to accomplish an effect by relying on a helper. If [you say] the hare's horn and the like are seen [to do so] here, [we answer:] no, because there is no example, as this is a matter in dispute. If [you say] the same [holds against us], [we answer:] no, because a real thing such as a pot is seen to have dependence.

## E 27,20

Nor is it proper that absence, which has no operation and is therefore not a real thing, should be a means of knowledge. For nothing in the world that is devoid of operation is seen to initiate an effect. Therefore the cognition of the absence of a knowable is produced by a means of knowledge that has the nature of an operation, because it has a knowable as its object, like the cognition of a pot and the like; likewise because [its object] is a real thing, since it is a cause of activity and cessation. The counter-instance is the hare's horn and the like; [the cognition of that] is not likewise produced by absence functioning as a means of knowledge, as one sees from the reason and the example just stated.

## E 27,25

[Formally:] the sight of the killer is the mark for the cognition of the victim's absence, because [that cognition] arises when it is present, like the cognition of smoke and the like, which is the cause of the cognition of fire and the like; and because it removes doubt. Or: the cognition of the victim's absence is produced by a mark, because it has the nature of a certainty, like the cognition of fire.

## E 28,1

[Objection:] "There is no pot here" is said not to be an inference, because no relation between mark and mark-bearer is grasped. For there is no search for a mark in "there is no pot here" in the way there is a search for the mark, smoke and the like, for the cognition of fire and the like, since the cognition arises even before any search for a mark.

## E 28,4

This is no fault, because the form of the cognition "here" is itself the mark. For when a cognition of the mark has arisen at the very outset, there is no search for a mark. The cognition coloured by the form "here" is the mark for the cognition of the absence of a pot or the like, [an absence] distinct from what colours [the cognition], just like the cognition of the killer. And here too the relation is one of opposition. Where, on the other hand, the colouring [contents] are not opposed, there is a cognition of existence.

## E 28,8

By this the inferential character of presumption too is explained, for there too the relation is one of invariable concomitance. For living Devadatta, when absent from the house, is outside; when he is present in the house, he is not outside. Thus there is inference from either. The example is being outside and absent from within, which is directly seen. For the cause of the apprehension of absence has [already] been stated to be [an object's] colouring or not colouring the mental function.

## E 28,13

In the case of fire and the like too, the apprehension of the capacity to burn and so on is nothing but inference, because the relation of invariable concomitance between an effect and what accomplishes it has been seen in oneself, in [one's own] arm and the like. For in one's inner self a capacity, which is a qualifier of the self, is apprehended just as the self is: "I am able to do this act", "I am unable". So the effect of burning and the like, too, is accomplished by a capacity: this is established by inference.

## E 28,17

[Objection:] But some proclaim that absence is perceptible. As, they say, when an existing thing is being grasped with the help of an illuminator such as a lamp, whatever is not perceived in that whole, [of that one cognizes] "it is not [there]". In this way the means of knowledge that illuminates what exists illuminates the non-existent as well. And they give this example: a man told "bring the unmarked garment" enters the storeroom, where marked and unmarked garments lie together, and by the very perception that grasps the marked garment perceives the garment qualified by the absence of a mark, and brings it. Therefore absence is perception and nothing else, since no other definition is possible.

## E 28,23

Not so, because it would follow that the ascertainment of an object by way of the form of the cognition is false. For it is accepted that [an object] has the nature that is being ascertained by perception in whatever form the cognition has; [otherwise], as in "the blue thing is yellow", so here too the cognition of the relation between qualifier and qualified would turn out to be false. Thus in "there is no pot here" there is a cognition of a relation between this place and the absence of a pot although no relation exists; for they do not admit a relation, whether of the nature of contact or the one called inherence, between the absence of a pot or the like and this place, as [there is] in "Devadatta is here". Therefore, like the cognition of absence, the cognition of the relation between qualifier and qualified in "the thing is blue" would also turn out to be false, because it is a cognition of a relation.

## E 29,4

And just as there is a cognition of a relation even though no relation exists between this place and the absence of a pot, in the same way even in a thing that is not blue, with no relation to blueness existing at all, a cognition of a relation to blue would follow; and likewise among [mere] relata. If, on the other hand, the relation really exists, then the relation's residing in two [relata] would be contradicted, because absence is admitted not to be a real thing.

## E 29,7

If, however, holding that "the meanings of the genitive are a hundred and one", one admitted a relation for as many things as there are meanings of the genitive, then [the doctrine] that relation is of just two kinds, called inherence and conjunction, would not hold.

## E 29,9

It might be said that even the hundred and one meanings of the genitive fall within those two. Not in the case of absence, because neither of the two is possible there. Moreover: in "there is no flower in the sky here", since the sky, the relatum, is also imperceptible, the absence of the sky-flower would turn out to be imperceptible. If [you say] it is indeed imperceptible, then it has followed that, like the absence of the sky-flower, the absence of a pot here on [this] spot of ground is imperceptible too. A difference must be stated.

## E 29,14

If [you say the difference lies in] the sky's being imperceptible, [we answer:] no. Since a falling flower is seen to exist in the sky, the sky's imperceptibility is no reason. Just as the sky's imperceptibility is no reason [against perceiving] the existence of a falling flower, so it would be no reason [against] grasping what is opposed to existence either. If [you say the reason is] simply the imperceptibility of space, then anything whatever, even something unrelated, such as the imperceptibility of what is hidden, would be a reason too. If [you say] the absence of the sky-flower is perceptible, then the uselessness of contact between sense and object would follow everywhere; and that being so, since there would be perception of what is not in contact, everyone would turn out to be omniscient.

## E 29,20

If [you say] that there it is inferred, then, since the absence of a pot here is, like the absence of the sky-flower, no different as an absence, its inferential character is precisely what is established. And this being so, since even for the upholder of absence as a means of knowledge the absence of fire and the like in hidden or distant water and the like is inferred, it is right to conclude that the absence of a pot here too, being of the same kind, is grasped by inference alone.

## E 29,24

**And the Vindhya is motionless, because it does not reach [another place]** is another formulation. "The Vindhya is motionless" is the thesis; "because it does not reach" is the reason; "like Caitra", like the crow's eye, is connected with both. With "the moon and stars possess motion" the connection is by similarity, with "and the Vindhya, because it does not reach" by dissimilarity. And this being so, there is no fault, [the connection] being made through the observation of positive and negative concomitance; or [rather], in this formulation there is no fault because of the observation of the converse of the negative [concomitance]. This has been set out. And because the definition is to be adapted to the particular thing defined, there is no fault.
